Starting a personal blog is something I’ve thought on and off about doing for… I don’t know… 15 years? As I was sifting through my old notes the other day, I found regular entries about blog post ideas I wanted to explore going back to when I was in undergrad, but somehow I never pulled it together between then and now.

What about this moment finally compelled me to start publicly writing?

For one, my partner has been [on an absolute tear lately with her writing](https://jessicafujimori.substack.com/), and it’s both inspirational and quite fun for us to hunker down writing together in the brief moments of opportunity that arise. For two, well, like all things in tech these days, the desire largely comes from the current AI-fueled zeitgeist, manifesting as some flavor of professional development opportunity with a sprinkling of countercultural frustration.

---

The upheaval of the software world has been making me think a lot about the different facets of software engineering, and the types of functions one does on the job. Depending on one’s particular role and organization, it’s not uncommon for engineers to spend time on anything of: designing systems, writing new code, refactoring existing code, being on-call, operating existing systems, sequencing work, team whiteboarding, mentoring a new hire, being mentored by a senior engineer, planning and plotting with PMs, coordinating with GTM, reporting to and working with senior leadership, watercooler conversations with folks on other teams, writing docs, writing blog posts, prepping a tech talk, running a product demo, traveling to a conference, hopping on a customer call, running interviews, tinkering, and generally, a lot of thinking.

I can personally vouch for having done all of these things at some point in time.{% sidenote() %}I didn’t even get to the difficult, heavy work of picking out a flavor of yogurt from the fridge every morning. This was hard for me in the days of office-yogurt, and even harder working from home now that I’ve stocked the fridge with too many options.{% end %}

While AI can assist in many of these areas, sometimes impressively so, I am doubtful it can evolve to replace the aspects that are fundamentally human. And there’s a lot that is fundamentally human — so many of these functions are centered on relationships, collaboration, and communication with other people. While the mix of tasks might ebb and flow over time, I have generally found my career to be much more human-centric than code-centric, maybe outside of the first 1-2 years on the job.

With such a heavy emphasis on people, so much of working well with others comes down to communicating well. Clear written work is then an immensely valuable tool for any form of formal, durable, or asynchronous communication, all common needs on the job. While writing is something I’ve always enjoyed, I am slooooow at it. Writing here, publicly, for me becomes practice to maybe get a little quicker in stringing words together, maybe get a little more comfortable working through the scary feeling that comes with sharing one’s work.{% sidenote() %}I still dread the day something I’ve done is posted on Hacker News.{% end %}

Another aspect that AI can never replace is the need for understanding. Software engineers cannot be effective without understanding the socio-technical system they’re working within: the people, the product, the technology that must all work in concert in order for their work to be useful. Despite the current capitalist zeal for everything to go faster faster faster with AI, developing understanding takes time.{% sidenote() %}If we knew how to make humans understand things faster, we probably wouldn’t settle for it taking ~25 years of education to add a new doctor to society.{% end %}

To build that understanding, or perhaps put it to the test, I find nothing more humbling than having to write down my thoughts. Writing is when and where all the uncertainty of my understanding is laid bare, and through the struggle of writing, where so much of my understanding becomes complete.

If I’m trying to share some idea in a conversation with a peer, I find I can get by with wildly imprecise communication. If I’m preparing a presentation, I spend more time in advance trying to get the language right, but can still often handwave through the uncertain parts. But when I really need to nail some thought with absolute precision, I need to write.

I don’t see any world in which communication and understanding aren’t essential to software engineering now and forever, and I see writing as one of the most fundamental instruments towards these goals.

---

So if these aspects of the work and writing are evergreen, why put up an old-school personal blog in 2026? Why right now?

While I find AI to be a useful tool for writing code, and I don’t mind it for cranking out relatively impersonal written work like reference documentation or design docs (so long as a human is willing to vouch for the work), I must say, at this point in time, I have come to despise AI-written content with all my heart.

The world needs more human connection today, not less, and AI writing is the ultimate peddler of fake connection. I might mind it less if the default AI voice was completely sterile — clearly non-human in its lack of voice. What I find so exhausting is the veneer of personality, the pastiche of persuasive hooks learned from too much of the worst of the social media influencer playbook in the training set. I am most pained when I see people present their own work, real work, wrapped up in the monotonous fakery of an LLM. It pains my soul.{% sidenote() %}Bryan Cantrill’s [Your intellectual fly is open](https://bcantrill.dtrace.org/2025/12/05/your-intellectual-fly-is-open/) is my favorite writing on the matter.{% end %}

In this moment, in our collective battle against the tides of slop, I feel that one of the things I can contribute is a small sliver of authentic work for those who seek it out. Maybe you’ll like it, maybe you won’t, but no matter what, you can trust it was written by me, Paul, an actual human.
